The PM2.5 in Singapore comes from many sources. Fires in Sumatra and Borneo make the big haze days. Ships, traffic, power stations, industry, homes and farms are always there. They make the background: the PM2.5 that the air brings on a normal day. Turn the layers on and off to see them.

📦 The box over Singapore, seen from the side

The model does not follow every particle. It follows one number: the PM2.5 in a box of air over Singapore. The box goes from the ground up to a lid. The lid is a warm layer of air: a temperature inversion. The lid goes down at night and up in the day. Press Play the day and look at the lid, the dots (particles) and the temperature line on the right.

Why is the PM2.5 often higher at night? The same pollution goes into a box that is 3 to 5 times thinner, so each cubic metre gets more particles. This is concentration = mass ÷ volume. Lid heights: forecast from the month-and-hour average of 2014–2025 (ERA5). Temperature line: a sketch of a typical day, not a measurement. Learn more about the lid.

Fires are not in this table. Fire smoke changes very much from day to day and from year to year. In a bad haze year, the fires make more smoke in a few weeks than all the sources in this table make in a year.

🌏 What is the background? Even with no fires, the air that comes to Singapore brings some PM2.5: sea salt, dust, and old pollution from the cities, industry, farms and ships of the whole region (the coloured layers on the map). The model gives it one number: about 8 µg/m³. It changes only a little with the path of the air.
